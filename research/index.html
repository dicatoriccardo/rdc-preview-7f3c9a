  • Seeds of Market-Based Capitalism: Financial Innovation and China’s Modernization Job Market Paper

    with Jiachen Li

    What explains the origin and takeoff of market-based capitalism in China?

    PDFSSRN
    Abstract

    This paper examines the historical and institutional roots of China’s market-based capitalism through the lens of financial development. We study how Christian missionaries contributed to the emergence of modern financial and economic activity between 1857 and 1936. We then trace how these effects became dormant under the planned economy after 1949 and re-emerged following the market-oriented reforms of the 1980s. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design for the pre-1936 period, we find that missionary activity increased modern banks and enterprises. We use newly compiled data on mission schools and publications, Chinese bankers, and elites to test several mechanisms. We find evidence mostly consistent with two: practical education and the diffusion of knowledge through publications and translations. Missionaries, especially Protestants, introduced training in subjects such as mathematics, bookkeeping, economics, and English, helping form high-skilled local actors and connect them to foreign knowledge and institutions. They also translated Western texts into vernacular Chinese, helping diffuse useful knowledge relevant to modern economic and financial practices. Exploiting the coincidental timing of railway construction relative to national missionary migration, we document that these effects weakened during the planned economy but re-emerged after China’s market reforms, shaping financial development and growth into recent decades. Banking infrastructure grew faster in less exposed areas between 1949 and 1978, suggesting that this re-emergence cannot be explained by physical capital. Instead, the return of high-skilled human capital and foreign investment help explain the re-emergence of these effects. Overall, the results suggest that missionaries transmitted not only human capital, but also institution-specific knowledge and transnational connections that helped plant the seeds of market-based capitalism in China.

  • Same Faith, Different Politics: Religious Broadcasting and Voting in Italy and Spain

    with Mattia Albertini

    Can religious identity shape elections?

    Abstract

    How does exposure to a common religious message translate into electoral behavior across different political contexts? We study Radio Maria, a Catholic broadcaster with a shared institutional mission, in Italy and Spain. We combine municipality-level election results with confidential transmitter histories and terrain-based signal propagation. Our identification exploits terrain-induced variation in radio reception, conditional on signal strength predicted by transmitter location and power, and compares electoral outcomes before and after Radio Maria’s expansion. In both countries, stronger reception shifts votes toward religiously aligned parties, but in different political directions. In Italy, exposure increases support for religious parties in the center, consistent with the historical location of Catholic political representation. In Spain, it instead increases support for the religious right. These findings show that religious media do not have a fixed ideological destination: the same underlying religious message can be translated differently depending on the political context in which voters receive it. More broadly, the contrast suggests that religious media can have very different implications for political polarization, potentially moderating it in one context while intensifying it in another.

  • Restricting Online Pornography and Violence Against Women: Evidence from India and the United States

    with Santiago Cantillo

    Does the adult film industry affect violence against women?

    Abstract

    Governments increasingly restrict online pornography in an effort to reduce violence against women. Yet the effect of such policies is theoretically ambiguous: pornography may increase aggression through exposure to degrading or violent content, but it may also displace behaviors that create opportunities for violence. We study two large restrictions with opposite effects. In India, a 2018 nationwide order blocking 827 websites increased reported rape and assault against women in districts with greater pre-ban mobile internet access. Our preferred instrumental-variables estimates imply increases of 1.25 and 2.89 annual incidents per 100,000 residents, respectively, while crimes not specifically directed at women do not rise. In the United States, state age-verification laws in 2023–2024 induced a major platform to withdraw from treated states. A staggered difference-in-differences design finds a decline of 1.32 monthly assaults against female victims per 100,000 residents, with no detectable change in rape or other sexual offenses. Both policies materially reduced access. Evidence on search behavior, offender age, and local gender and household environments suggests that the balance between exposure and behavioral substitution differs across settings. The results show that pornography restrictions need not have a common effect on violence against women and highlight the importance of user adjustment and local context in evaluating digital regulation.

  • Religious Broadcasting and Belief Formation in Tanzania

    with Mattia Albertini

    Are religious media effective at influencing beliefs and civic engagement?

    Abstract

    Can mass religious broadcasting change religious identity and social behavior? We study the expansion of Radio Maria across Tanzania between 2001 and 2024. We combine the staggered rollout of the network’s transmitters with predicted signal strength, individual-level data from Afrobarometer and the Demographic and Health Surveys, and geocoded conflict data from ACLED. Our empirical strategy compares locations with similar proximity to transmitters but different signal reception because of local topography, while accounting for free-space signal, other radio coverage, and location and year fixed effects. Exposure to Radio Maria increases Catholic affiliation and reduces Protestant affiliation, with larger effects in places farther from historical Catholic missions. It also reduces in-person contact with religious leaders, consistent with radio partly substituting for traditional religious institutions. We find little change in most social and intergroup attitudes, but some reduction in conventional political engagement and an increase in protests and religiously connected events. These additional events are not accompanied by more fatalities or violence against civilians. The results show how religious media can reshape affiliation while also affecting civic mobilization.

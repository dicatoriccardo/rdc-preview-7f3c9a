  • Seeds of Market-Based Capitalism: Christian Missionaries and China’s Modernization (Job Market Paper)

    with Jiachen Li

    Did Christian missionaries contribute to China’s modernization by spreading market-based institutions?

    PDFSSRN
    Abstract

    This paper examines the historical and institutional roots of China’s market-based capitalism through the lens of financial development. We study how Christian missionaries facilitated the adoption of modern banks and the establishment of enterprises between 1857 and 1936, and how this historical exposure interacted with the market-oriented reforms of the 1980s to shape growth through 2022. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design for the pre-1936 period, we find that missionary presence increased modern banks by 31% and enterprises by 27%. Our evidence points to two mechanisms: practical education and the diffusion of useful knowledge through publications and translations. Missionaries, especially Protestants, introduced training in subjects such as mathematics, bookkeeping, economics, and English, helping local actors operate modern financial institutions. They also translated Western texts into vernacular Chinese, helping diffuse useful knowledge relevant to modern economic and financial practices. Using an instrumental variable strategy for long-run exposure, we document that these effects weakened between 1949 and 1978 but re-emerged after the market reforms. Overall, the results suggest that missionaries transmitted not only human capital, but also institution-specific knowledge that helped plant the seeds of market-based capitalism in China.

    Presentations: Boston University (2024); University of Bologna (2025); Xiamen University Economic History Conference (2025); ASREC Annual Conference (2025, 2026); HHDI–NIDE Joint Workshop, Queen’s University Belfast (2026); 30th Annual SIOE Conference, INSEAD (2026).

  • Religious Broadcasting and Politics in Italy

    with Mattia Albertini

    Does Catholic radio affect voting and political behavior in Italy?

    Abstract

    Does religious broadcasting influence political behavior even when it does not explicitly endorse a political party? We study Radio Maria, a Catholic radio network that expanded throughout Italy after 1987. We combine municipality-level parliamentary election returns with predicted radio signal strength, antenna locations, party classifications, and measures of broadcast content. Our empirical strategy exploits differences in signal propagation generated by local topography, while accounting for free-space signal strength, municipality characteristics, province-by-year shocks, and exposure to commercial television. Greater exposure to Radio Maria increases support for religious and centrist parties, while modestly reducing support for parties on both the left and the right. It also appears to reduce ideological polarization. These effects emerged as Christian Democracy, the traditional political home of Italian Catholics, was disappearing. The findings suggest that religious media can preserve and reorganize religious political identity even in the absence of direct partisan endorsements, creating a centrist Catholic political channel after the collapse of explicitly confessional parties.

  • Religious Broadcasting and Belief Formation in Tanzania

    with Mattia Albertini

    Does Catholic radio drive religious conversion and reshape intergroup relations in Tanzania?

    Abstract

    Can mass religious broadcasting change religious identity and social behavior? We study the expansion of Radio Maria across Tanzania between 2001 and 2024. We combine the staggered rollout of the network’s transmitters with predicted signal strength, individual-level data from Afrobarometer and the Demographic and Health Surveys, and geocoded conflict data from ACLED. Our empirical strategy compares locations with similar proximity to transmitters but different signal reception because of local topography, while accounting for free-space signal, other radio coverage, and location and year fixed effects. Exposure to Radio Maria increases Catholic affiliation and reduces Protestant affiliation, with larger effects in places farther from historical Catholic missions. It also reduces in-person contact with religious leaders, consistent with radio partly substituting for traditional religious institutions. We find little change in most social and intergroup attitudes, but some reduction in conventional political engagement and an increase in protests and religiously connected events. These additional events are not accompanied by more fatalities or violence against civilians. The results show how religious media can reshape affiliation while also affecting civic mobilization.
